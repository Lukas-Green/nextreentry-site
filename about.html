About

We are not studying this population. We came out of it.

Every person building Better Neighbors has been incarcerated. That is not a diversity statement. It is the qualification, and it is the one thing in this sector that cannot be hired for.

Anecdotal experience is not a lesser kind of knowledge here. It is the primary source.

A second chance needs more than a release date.

Better Neighbors helps people leaving prison and addiction rebuild their lives through art, education, and a community that expects them to make it. Three pillars, and they still hold.

Flagship program

Art Inside Out

Kirk Charlton's twelve lesson art program, built during his years inside Eastern Oregon Correctional Institution. You do not erase the wrong line, you read it. That is a drawing lesson and a life lesson wearing the same clothes.

Second pillar

Reentry support and education

Practical help and self directed skills for the first hard months out. This is where our fourteen lessons live, and it is the pillar lived experience changes most.

Third pillar

Community and belonging

Members, mentors and neighbours who turn a release into a real return home.

Reentry is not a personal failing. It is a design problem.

That sentence is the reason this is not built like a charity for people who failed. When somebody comes home to no identification, no address, no phone, no credit file and a supervision calendar that started before they did, the outcome is not a character result. It is what the design produces.

Repair, not rescue. We do not fix people. We help them rebuild, balance and belong. The strength comes from the mending.

Knowing the system is not the same as having lived in it.

There are good people in reentry work who have read everything written about this population. They run programs, they write grants, they mean it. And there is still a gap between studying a thing and waking up inside it for years, which is exactly where the advice goes thin.

A degree teaches you what the research found. It does not teach you what it feels like to hand over a form with a gap on it, or how a room changes when somebody finds out. It cannot tell you which piece of standard advice will get somebody violated.

We have both halves. We did the years, and then we went and learned the book side on top of it so we could be useful to people in our own position. School of hard knocks is literally us. That combination is not something another organisation can go out and acquire.

Everything we publish carries this mark. It means it was written by someone who has been through it.

You are not the worst thing you did. You are also not excused from it.

Nothing here excuses or removes culpability or responsibility for the harm this population has caused. This is not denial, and it is not erasing ownership of hurtful actions and decisions. People did real damage. They own it.

What we refuse is the part where the person permanently becomes the decision. The layers of shame run deep, and society fails badly by keeping the identity welded to the act, very often something done when someone was young who is now walking out decades later. The version of a person who committed a crime and the version who comes home are frequently not remotely the same person.

So the work is two things at once. Teach people that the past will surface for the rest of their lives, and teach them how to move forward anyway, without pretending the record is not there and without letting it be the only fact about them.

Everyone coming home, not just the men.

The released population skews male, and most programs quietly write for that majority and let everyone else read around it. We are not doing that.

Women leaving incarceration face the same conditions, supervision and stigma, plus a set of pressures the male majority does not carry. Writing this curriculum for men and telling women it also applies is not the same as building it for both. Where the experience differs, we say so instead of flattening it.

If you are coming home, this is for you. That sentence has no asterisk on it.

Transitional education based on what is actually true right now.

Someone who went in during one decade and came out in another is not behind on one thing. They are behind on the whole texture of daily life, and that texture keeps moving. Advice that was correct four years ago can now be useless or actively dangerous.

01

We research constantly

Scams, platforms, financial products, hiring practice and the tools everyone suddenly expects you to know. All of it moves.

02

We rewrite, not append

When something changes, the lesson gets rebuilt. Stale material with a new paragraph bolted on is how programs quietly go wrong.

03

We date what we publish

Anyone reading a lesson should know when it was last checked. Undated advice is a claim about the present that nobody has verified.

What we are not.

  • Not a treatment provider. We are not licensed clinicians and we do not deliver therapy. We teach people how to find and use one, which is a different job.
  • Not a legal service. We do not give legal advice or interpret anyone's conditions of supervision. Your conditions are yours and your attorney and officer are the authorities on them.
  • Not a placement agency. We do not promise housing or a job. We teach the ground truth about how both actually work with a record.
  • Not neutral about honesty. We will tell someone the barrier is real rather than sell them optimism they will crash into in month four.

Who we want to work with.

A curriculum only goes as far as the doors that open at the end of it.

Employers

People willing to read a person before a record, and to say plainly what they can and cannot hire for.

Property managers

Housing is the hardest door and the one most often closed silently. Honest criteria help more than a maybe.

Facilities and supervision

Anyone who wants something real to hand a person on the way out instead of a pamphlet.

Clinicians

Therapists who understand trauma, neurodivergence and this population, and are taking referrals.
